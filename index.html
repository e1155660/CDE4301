1. Objective and Context

1.1 Objective

The main objective of our project is to automate a critical task in the medication restocking process of the Drug Dispensing System 2 (DDS-2) at National University Hospital (NUH) Pharmacy. Specifically, our project aims to reduce the time required for medication restocking and minimise human errors by developing a solution capable of automatically counting medication blisters and recognising and verifying medication information, such as batch numbers and expiry dates. By reducing reliance on repetitive manual counting and data entry, the proposed solution aims to improve the efficiency, accuracy and reliability of the medication restocking process while maintaining patient safety.

1.2 Context

The Drug Dispensing System 2 (DDS-2, Video 1), developed by GHT, a Singapore-based healthcare company, is an automated medication dispensing system used at National University Hospital (NUH) Pharmacy.

Video 1: DDS-2 dispensing system at NUH Pharmacy

There are two main phases we identified in the operation of DDS-2:

1. Restocking phase
2. Dispensing phase

The dispensing phase is fully automated, using a robotic arm equipped with a suction mechanism to pick up medication blisters and place it on the dispensing tray. However, the system still requires manual intervention during the restocking phase, making the process time-consuming and susceptible to human error.

1.2.1 Restocking Phase

The medication restocking phase consists of three main steps (Figure 1). In the first step, the staff prepare the medication blisters, count and load them into the cartridges in the correct orientation. In the second step, the information such as the batch number and expiry date is manually entered into the DDS-2 interface. The blisters are recounted, and staff randomly sample three to four blisters to cross-check against the entered information. Finally, before the cartridges are loaded into the machine, a pharmacist verifies the entered information and recounts the blisters. Each step is performed by different personnel, with the final verification always conducted by a pharmacist. Errors at any step require the entire process to be repeated. A detailed process flow chart in in Appendix A.

The results, presented in Table 2, indicate that the process takes approximately eight minutes per cartridge. Although this may not seem significant for a single cartridge, the time accumulates considerably when staff have to restock 50 - 60 cartridges throughout the day which could take more than 8 hours a day. This places a considerable burden on pharmacy staff, with one staff member at NUH pharmacy noting that he feels "burnt out after counting 10 cartridges a day." While these repetitive checks are essential for patient safety, they also increase the time required for the restocking process.

1.2.2 Error Analysis

We investigated the relationship between staff experience and DDS-2 error rates using the raw error data provided by the pharmacists. We analysed the error rates of all staff members who had restocked the cartridge more than 9 times. Our analysis revealed considerable variability in error rates across different levels of experience (Figure 2). Although more experienced staff might be expected to make fewer errors, no statistically significant relationship was established. This suggests that experience alone may not be sufficient to eliminate errors, highlighting the importance of improving the process itself.

To identify areas requiring the greatest attention, we then analysed the frequency of different restocking errors. As shown in Figure 3, certain errors occurred more frequently than others, allowing us to prioritise the problems that our proposed solution should address rather than attempting to automate every aspect of the process. We identified three important error categories: incorrect quantities and incorrect batch numbers and incorrect expiry date. Incorrect quantities may arise during the manual counting of medication blisters, while incorrect batch numbers and expiry date may result from manual data entry or verification. These findings directly inform the development of our prototype, something that reduces the likelihood of counting and verification errors made by the pharmacists.

The consequences of these errors extend beyond the additional time spent when the restocking process is repeated. Medication errors like these can have serious consequences when they reach patients, potentially resulting in additional hospitalisation costs, estimated here at approximately S$3,650 per error (Ng et al., 2020). Therefore, improving medication verification is not only about increasing operational efficiency but also about reducing risks to patients and healthcare institutions.


2. Scope, Problem Statement and Existing Technology

2.1. Scope

2.1.1 Stakeholder's Analysis

In order to understand who would be affected by our proposed solution, we first conducted a stakeholder analysis, categorising our stakeholders into four key groups based on their levels of influence and interest in the project. From this, we identified the pharmacists and hospital operations team as our key players, given their significant involvement in and influence over the DDS-2 workflow. Patients and caregivers are not directly involved in the restocking process and remain as indirect stakeholders. Any improvements in efficiency and medication safety could ultimately benefit them but they have significantly lower interest and influence compared to our key players. The hospital IT team, on the other hand, plays an important role in approving the implementation of any automated solution, although their limited involvement in the daily workflow means they have comparatively lower interest. Meanwhile, pharmacy technicians, who are responsible for carrying out Steps 1 and 2 of the restocking process, have a high level of interest in any improvements made, but less influence over implementation decisions compared to pharmacists and the hospital operations team. Finally, we also considered home-visit nurses, as the hospital operations team hopes that any automation developed could eventually be extended beyond the pharmacy. These nurses face similar challenges in counting blisters and verifying medication details, and could therefore benefit from a more accurate, automated approach. However, since they fall outside the immediate scope of our project, their current interest and influence remain relatively low, making them a potential consideration for future development.

With the stakeholders identified, we ultimately decided to focus primarily on the pharmacists and hospital operations team, as they stand to benefit most directly from improvements to the workflow. For the hospital operations team, a more streamlined and automated restocking process could enable better manpower allocation and more effective pharmacy planning. For pharmacists, who already juggle multiple responsibilities in a high-pressure environment, reducing the time spent manually verifying cartridge information would allow them to redirect their time and energy towards other essential duties.

2.1.1 User Survey

To ensure that our solution addresses the challenges faced by those working directly with the system, we needed to understand their experiences firsthand. We therefore conducted a survey among NUH pharmacy staff to gather diverse perspectives and identify their most pressing needs. The response rate was 33% with 22 responses. One of the questions we asked was, "Which stages of restocking were most difficult?" The results revealed that counting was, by far, the most challenging step, largely due to the repetitive nature and high volume of the restocking workflow. This was followed by blister information verification, blister orientation, and database entry. These findings helped us identify the areas where improvements would have the greatest impact.

Having identified the most challenging stages, the next question was: What improvements do the users actually want? (Figure 6)To find out, we asked respondents which steps they would find most useful to automate. Once again, counting emerged as the top priority, while blister information verification and orientation also ranked highly. This alignment between the most difficult tasks and the most desired areas for automation highlights a clear demand for a solution that can reduce the burden of manual cartridge restocking. However, when we asked respondents about their greatest concerns regarding a proposed solution, accuracy was the most frequently cited issue. This reveals an important consideration: while users want greater automation, it cannot come at the expense of accuracy. Our solution must therefore reduce repetitive manual work while maintaining, or ideally improving, the reliability of the existing process.

Beyond improving the daily workflow for pharmacy staff, automation could also bring broader operational benefits through more efficient resource allocation and cost-effective pharmacy planning. According to the NUHS Centre for Healthcare Innovation, the projected annual manpower savings from pharmacy automation could reach up to S$3.3 million per year and a 2.5-fold reduction in supply-related medication errors (Centre for Healthcare Innovation, 2026). Although these figures reflect the broader potential of pharmacy automation rather than our specific solution, they highlight the significant value that streamlining manual processes can bring. By automating the cartridge restocking phase, we therefore aim to address challenges at both levels: reducing the workload and stress experienced by pharmacists and pharmacy technicians, while also supporting the hospital operations team in optimising manpower and resources.

2.2. Problem Statement

We defined the problem statement for our project from the perspective of the pharmacists and hospital operations team, who are the primary stakeholders in the DDS-2 restocking process. The problem statement is as follows:

Despite stringent medication-safety requirements, NUH Pharmacy’s manual DDS-2 cartridge replenishment workflow is vulnerable to counting and medication verification errors due to human workload, visual limitations, label clarity, and packaging variation.


2.3. Existing Technology

2.3.1 JVM VIXEN EX

The JVM VIXEN EX is an automated medication inspection system that uses imaging technology to extract and verify patient and medication information printed on packaged medication pouches. It also incorporates an error-identification mechanism that marks incorrectly packaged medication for re-inspection, providing a useful reference for the proposed rejection system.

However, the VIXEN EX is designed to inspect medication after pouch packaging rather than intact manufacturer blister packs. It does not support automated blister counting or verification of batch numbers and expiry dates printed directly on blister foil. Consequently, it does not fully address the requirements of the DDS-2 cartridge replenishment workflow.

2.3.2 Pharmablister Countmaster

The Pharmablister Countmaster integrates automated blister counting, visual defect detection, and rejection mechanisms to improve pharmacy efficiency and inventory accuracy. Its combination of blister singulation, inspection, and counting provides a relevant reference for the proposed solution.

However, its rejection mechanism primarily identifies physically defective blister packs, such as those with missing tablets or damaged packaging, rather than incorrect batch numbers or expiry dates. Furthermore, it does not incorporate OCR-based extraction and verification of printed blister information. As these functions are essential to the DDS-2 workflow, the Countmaster alone is insufficient to meet the project's requirements.

2.3.3 SEA Vision Harle2D

The SEA Vision Harle2D is an automated pharmaceutical inspection system that utilises Optical Character Recognition (OCR), Optical Character Verification (OCV), and specialised lighting to verify printed medication information, including batch numbers and expiry dates. Its use of controlled illumination to minimise reflections from aluminium blister foil provides a relevant reference for the proposed solution's imaging and verification system.

However, the Harle2D is primarily designed for integration into pharmaceutical manufacturing lines rather than hospital pharmacy restocking workflows. It does not specifically address automated blister counting, individual blister handling, or cartridge replenishment. Therefore, despite its relevant information verification capabilities, it does not provide a complete solution for the DDS-2 workflow

2.3.4 Jornen Desblister Machine DBM80

The Jornen DBM80 is designed for removing medication from blister packaging. Its adjustable feeding mechanism, bottom-feed singulation, and vertical magazine feeder provide useful references for handling blister packs of varying dimensions while maintaining individual blister alignment.

However, the DBM80 is intended for deblistering rather than inspecting and restocking intact blister packs. It does not incorporate automated blister counting, imaging-based information extraction, or verification of batch numbers and expiry dates. Consequently, its mechanical features may inform the proposed blister feeding mechanism, but additional inspection and verification systems would be required to meet the DDS-2 workflow requirements.

2.3.5 Summary of Existing Technology

Ultimately, while each of these three technologies offers valuable features that we can learn from, none provides a complete solution to the specific challenges faced during NUH Pharmacy's DDS-2 cartridge replenishment workflow. This highlights the opportunity for us to integrate their relevant strengths into a solution that can accurately count, verify, and handle blisters without damaging their packaging, while addressing the needs and concerns identified by our stakeholders.



3. Design

3.1. Design Objectives and Requirements

Our proposed OptIVS design aims to improve the efficiency and reliability of restocking medication blisters into the existing DDS-2 system at NUH Pharmacy. The design focuses on reducing repetitive manual counting and verification tasks while ensuring medication safety and minimising disruption to existing pharmacy operations.

The design requirements were established based on stakeholder feedback, workflow observations and the limitations of the existing restocking process (Detailed project timelline in Appendix C). The primary functional requirements include automated blister counting, verification of batch numbers, expiry dates and medication details, detection of inconsistent blister orientation, and automatic rejection of non-conforming blisters. A target accuracy of approximately 99.99% was established for counting and information extraction, to match the requirement of verifying the drugs accurately. Additionally, the system must accommodate different blister dimensions, prevent damage to medication packaging and operate within the time constraints of the existing workflow.

The design adopts the poka-yoke principle, which emphasises mistake-proofing through mechanisms that prevent or detect errors without relying solely on operator attention. According to Saurin et al. (2012), poka-yoke devices can incorporate control functions that interrupt operations or automatically remove defective items, alongside warning functions that alert operators to abnormalities. By integrating automated inspection, controlled blister handling and a rejection mechanism, OptIVS applies these principles to identify and separate non-conforming blisters, reducing reliance on manual verification while supporting consistent and repeatable operation.

3.2. Concept Generation and Development

A morphological chart (Figure 11) was developed to systematically explore solutions for the functions of OptIVS. These functions included blister singulation, transportation, image acquisition, text extraction, counting, information verification and rejection. Multiple mechanisms were considered for each function before being combined into two design concepts: a vertical drop-through system and a horizontal conveyor system.

The vertical drop-through concept utilises a spring-driven pusher to release individual blisters from a horizontally arranged magazine. Each blister passes through an imaging region under gravity, where two high-speed cameras capture the medication information before the blister enters a collection tray.

The horizontal conveyor concept employs a controlled feeding mechanism to transfer single blisters onto a moving conveyor belt. The blisters travel through an inspection region, where a camera captures images for information extraction and verification before the system decides whether to accept or reject them.

We evaluated both concepts to determine their suitability for integration into the proposed automated medication verification workflow, which is explained in Section 4.3 and evaluated in Section 5.3.



4. Testing

During the interim phase, testing focused on two OptIVS subsystems, including image acquisition and text recognition, and blister presentation. The first assessed whether blister information could be captured and recognised while the second explored how individual blisters could be presented to a camera. The subsystems were tested separately, so the results do not represent the performance of an integrated prototype.

4.1 Image Acquisition Testing

4.1.1 Lighting Assessment

A preliminary assessment investigated the effects of lighting position on image quality using an adjustable cardboard enclosure and two USB spotlights. Illumination from above, parallel to the blister surface, and diagonally from below was evaluated visually based on glare, contrast, and embossed-text legibility.

Samples included medication types identified in NUH pharmacy error statistics and those obtained from team members' households: Amlodipine 10 mg, Paracil 500 mg, Senna 7.5 mg, Glyprin 100 mg/45 mg, Zenpro 20 mg, Diabetmin 850 mg, Rozuva FC 10 mg, and Uphamol 500 mg (Figure 12).

4.1.2 Camera Comparison

The Raspberry Pi and IDS U3-36L0XC cameras were each positioned at the top of the same enclosure and compared at different working distances. Image clarity was assessed visually, while preliminary recognition experiments were conducted using a pretrained OCR model from Hugging Face. Only the visual assessment is reported in this subsection to distinguish the preliminary camera comparison from the systematic evaluation of OCR pipelines presented in Section 4.2.

4.2 Text Extraction

Text extraction experiments assessed recognition of batch numbers and expiry dates on blister packaging. Three OCR engines were compared using whole-image OCR and You-Only-Look-Once (YOLO)-assisted text localisation.

4.2.1 Data Set and Test Targets

The dataset comprised 429 images across eight blister types, divided into 297 training, 87 validation, and 45 test images. A 70:20:10 split was targeted within each brand, with temporally adjacent captures grouped to reduce data leakage. Training incorporated flipping, scaling, and colour augmentation, with a maximum of 65 epochs and early stopping after 15 epochs without improvement.

The OCR comparison used 30 test images containing 92 annotated text regions (46 batch numbers and 46 expiry dates). All pipelines were evaluated against the same target set. As some images contained multiple targets, these regions were not statistically independent. The test images came from brands represented in the dataset and hence performance on unseen brands remains unknown in this interim.

4.2.2 OCR and YOLO Configurations

Each of the three OCR engines, PaddleOCR, EasyOCR, and Tesseract, was evaluated in four pipeline configurations: a whole-image baseline and three configurations using YOLO-oriented bounding box (YOLO-OBB) localisation (Table 3). In the whole-image baseline, OCR was applied directly to the complete blister image. In the YOLO-assisted configurations, detected text regions were cropped and rectified before being passed to the OCR engine. The three YOLO configurations differed in how the annotated regions were classified.

Both approaches produced text predictions that were compared with reference transcriptions. For whole-image OCR, recognised text was assigned to annotated target fields for scoring. For YOLO-assisted OCR, predictions were obtained from the detected crops and compared with the corresponding target fields (Figure 13).

4.2.3 Evaluation Metrics and Scoring

The pipelines were assessed using character accuracy, exact-match accuracy, detection coverage and processing time. Predictions and references were normalised to uppercase and non-alphanumeric characters were removed before comparison.

Character accuracy was calculated using pooled character edit distance:


\[ \text{Character Accuracy} = \max\left(0, 1 - \frac{\text{Total character edit distance}} {\text{Total number of reference characters}} \right) \times 100\% \]


This measure reflects only partial text recognition, whereas exact-match accuracy is the proportion of target fields for which the complete normalised prediction matches the reference. Exact matching is relevant to batch numbers and expiry dates because one incorrect character may make a value unsuitable for verification. Detection coverage is the proportion of annotated target fields matched to a YOLO detection under the applicable matching criteria. It measures localisation but not whether the text was read correctly.

For whole-image OCR, annotated target regions were used to assign recognised text to the corresponding batch-number or expiry-date field for scoring. An OCR region was assigned to a target when at least 50% of its area lay within the annotated region (Nayef et al., 2017). All 92 targets remained in the evaluation denominator, including those for which no text was recognised.

Processing time was measured separately across 30 images containing the 92 targets. The 12 pipeline configurations were evaluated on a CPU, reporting mean and standard deviation in seconds per image. For whole-image OCR, timing only includes OCR on the full image while for YOLO-assisted pipelines, it covers detection, crop rectification and OCR of eligible detected regions. Image loading and one-time model initialisation were excluded. These measurements do not represent the complete time required by an integrated prototype.

4.2.4 Additional Investigation

The three-class YOLO model was manually evaluated across all 92 text targets, categorising detections as complete crops, partial crops, or misses to identify localisation errors affecting OCR.

4.3 Blister Presentation Concept Evaluation

Two blister presentation concepts were evaluated: a vertical-drop mechanism using side-mounted cameras to capture information on falling blisters, and a horizontal conveyor transporting individual blisters past a camera (Figures 14 and 15(a)). A preliminary conveyor prototype was developed to explore image presentation (Figure 15(b)).

Both concepts were compared using verification accuracy, singulation reliability, packaging safety, ease of operation, error-proofing, and implementation feasibility, reflecting NUH pharmacy staff concerns regarding blister handling and verification. The comparison informed concept selection in Section 5.3.





5. Analysis

5.1 Image Acquisition

5.1.1 Lighting

Lighting position affected text visibility. Direct illumination produced glare where the aluminium surface reflected light towards the camera, making some embossed characters harder to distinguish (Figure 16). Redirecting the spotlights towards the enclosure walls provided more diffuse illumination, reducing visible glare and improving text contrast (Figure 17). This arrangement was subsequently adopted for image acquisition.

However, improvements were assessed visually rather than through OCR accuracy measurements. Further controlled testing across different blister materials and embossing styles is required before lighting design is finalised.

5.1.2 Camera Comparison

Some small text remained unclear in Raspberry Pi camera images even after the blister was raised on platforms. The IDS camera was therefore evaluated in the same enclosure. In the examples assessed, its images appeared sharper and text remained in focus at a greater working distance (Figures 17 and 18). The IDS camera was subsequently selected to collect images for model development and testing.

As the comparison was primarily visual, improvements in OCR accuracy cannot yet be attributed to the camera change. Further evaluation under controlled working distances, exposure, and focus settings is required.

5.2 Text Extraction Performance

Among the whole-image OCR engines, PaddleOCR achieved the highest character accuracy at 68.1%, compared with 21.0% for EasyOCR and 0.2% for Tesseract (Figure 19). Whole-image PaddleOCR achieved 47.8% exact-match accuracy, corresponding to 44 of the 92 batch-number and expiry-date targets after normalisation (Figure 20).

These metrics answer different questions. Character accuracy measures the pooled similarity of recognised text to the references; exact match requires the complete normalised field to be correct. A prediction can therefore contain several correct characters but still fail exact-label matching.

PaddleOCR was the strongest whole-image baseline, but further improvement is needed. Ground-truth regions were used to assign OCR output to fields for evaluation, so this result does not establish that the system can independently identify and assign every field in deployment, especially when encountering unseen blister images. PaddleOCR was selected for further investigation based on the interim comparison.

5.2.2 Effect of YOLO-assisted Cropping

For PaddleOCR, the one-, two- and three-class YOLO configurations achieved character accuracies of 20.8%, 43.2% and 35.6%, respectively, compared with 68.1% for whole-image PaddleOCR. Although the two-class configuration performed best among YOLO-assisted pipelines, localisation and cropping did not improve recognition accuracy under the tested conditions.

Nguyen et al. (2026) reported a YOLO-OBB and OCR approach for engraved serial numbers on metallic surfaces using orientation handling and post-processing (Figure 21). These methods suggest directions for investigation although the reported performance is not directly comparable because the datasets and test conditions differ.

5.2.3 Three-class Detection and Crop Quality

A missed or incomplete crop can prevent the OCR engine from receiving the full target text (Figure 22). Glare, low contrast and orientation are also possible contributors that require further investigation.

Manual review found 55 complete crops, four partial crops and 33 misses among the 92 targets (Figure 23). Thus, 59 targets were detected, contributing 64.1% of detection coverage. Although 55 of the 59 detected targets were fully captured, the 33 missed targets could not proceed through the intended OCR pathway.

These findings indicate that detection coverage remains a major limitation, requiring improvements in annotation consistency, dataset representation, and handling of challenging images.

5.2.4 Processing Time and Accuracy Trade-off

The processing time experiment showed a trade-off between speed and recognition accuracy (Figure 24). For PaddleOCR, whole-image processing achieved 68.1% character accuracy but took approximately 40 seconds per image on the CPU. The two-class YOLO-assisted pipeline achieved a lower character accuracy of 43.2% but took approximately 2 seconds per image. In particular, missed detections reduce the number of regions passed to OCR and may reduce processing time. Future work should improve detection coverage while retaining the time benefit of YOLO cropping.

5.3 Blister Presentation Concept Analysis

The vertical-drop and horizontal-conveyor concepts were compared using a qualitative decision matrix based on preliminary observations and pharmacy staff feedback (Table 4). The scores represent relative design judgements rather than measured performance.

The horizontal conveyor was preferred due to its more controlled blister positioning and greater flexibility in image-capture timing. In contrast, the vertical-drop mechanism presented challenges in controlling blisters of varying dimensions, and OCR failed to detect text during preliminary drop testing (Figure 25).

NUH pharmacy staff also raised concerns about releasing multiple blisters together and applying excessive pressure that could damage packaging or dislodge tablets. Staff preferred verification of every blister to random sampling. Singulation, consistent image capture and packaging safety are consequently important design requirements.

Hence, the horizontal conveyor was selected for further development. Next steps include adjusting guide rails and contact surfaces for different blister sizes, limiting feeding force and inspecting packaging after repeated trials.

5.4 Limitations and Next Steps

The best-performing interim pipeline, whole-image PaddleOCR, achieved 68.1% character accuracy and 47.8% exact-match accuracy on the 92 targets. However, the limited test sample, absence of unseen-brand evaluation, and independent subsystem testing restrict the generalisability of these results. Further validation is required to determine whether the proposed 99.99% verification accuracy target is achievable.

Future work will optimise illumination using ring lights, LED strips, and diffusers, while evaluating camera resolution, focus, and capture speed under dynamic conditions. OCR improvements will focus on annotation consistency, additional training data, orientation handling, and post-processing. Model-assisted annotation may also be explored, subject to manual verification.

Following subsystem integration, the complete prototype will be evaluated for verification accuracy, processing time, singulation reliability, and packaging safety across different blister types. Its performance and operational efficiency will then be compared against the existing NUH pharmacy restocking workflow.


6. Prototype Design and Development

6.1. Overall Prototype Architecture

Following the concept evaluation, the horizontal conveyor system was developed into the first OptIVS prototype. The proposed design integrates a vertical blister magazine, an individual blister feeding mechanism, a horizontal conveyor, a camera-based inspection region and separate collection areas for accepted and rejected blisters (Figure 26).

The vertical magazine stores blisters in a stacked arrangement to facilitate sequential feeding. A mechanical pusher transfers individual blisters from the magazine onto the conveyor, reducing the need for operators to manually position each blister for inspection.

The conveyor transports the blister through a designated imaging region, where the camera captures the medication packaging for text extraction. The extracted information is compared against a reference to identify discrepancies before the blister is directed towards the appropriate collection area.

6.2. Blister Feeding and Transportation Mechanism

The blister feeding mechanism is designed to achieve singulation, whereby only one blister is released per feeding cycle. This is necessary to prevent overlapping blisters, which could obstruct the camera's field of view and compromise verification accuracy.

The proposed mechanism uses a controlled pusher to transfer the lowest available blister from the vertical magazine onto the conveyor. Following each feeding cycle, the remaining blister stack advances into position for subsequent dispensing. This provides a controlled transportation path between the feeding mechanism and the inspection region.

6.3. Inspection and Rejection Mechanism

The inspection subsystem is intended to operate in conjunction with the mechanical transportation system. As each blister reaches the imaging region, the camera captures the packaging surface for text extraction of the batch number, expiry date and pill details.

The prototype design also incorporates a proposed rejection mechanism to separate blisters that fail verification. When a mismatch is detected, the affected blister is intended to be diverted into a rejection area instead of proceeding towards the accepted collection area.

6.4. Proposed Operating Mechanism

The proposed OptIVS operating sequence (Video 2) begins with the operator restocking blisters into the vertical magazine in the required orientation. Before continuous operation, the system verifies the first three blisters for information and alignment in the inspection region. If their medication details and alignment are consistent, the system establishes these readings as the reference baseline. Otherwise, the system stops operation and alerts the operator to investigate the discrepancy.

Once the baseline is established, the feeding mechanism transfers individual blisters onto the horizontal conveyor. Each blister is transported through the inspection region, where images are acquired and processed to extract the relevant medication information.

The extracted batch number, expiry date and medication details are compared against the baseline. The system also checks whether the blister alignment is consistent with the reference.

Blisters satisfying the verification conditions are permitted to proceed towards the accepted collection tray. Conversely, blisters containing inconsistent information or alignment are directed towards the rejection area.

This sequence is repeated until the required quantity of blisters has been processed, then presents the data on a display. By combining sequential feeding, automated inspection and rejection, the proposed system aims to reduce manual counting and verification requirements while introducing an additional error-detection mechanism into the medication restocking process.

6.5. Limitations and Future Improvements

Although the initial prototype establishes the proposed mechanical architecture, several limitations require further investigation. Variations in blister dimensions and thickness may affect feeding consistency, while insufficient mechanical clearance could result in jamming or packaging damage. Furthermore, the inspection and rejection mechanisms must be synchronised to ensure that non-conforming blisters are correctly identified and separated.

More importantly, the text recognition accuracy of the inspection subsystem must approach the target of 99.99%, as the extracted information directly determines the system's subsequent actions. Inaccurate recognition of batch numbers, expiry dates or medication details could result in the incorrect acceptance or rejection of blister strips, compromising the reliability and safety of the overall system. Therefore, further improvements to the camera setup, lighting conditions and OCR model are essential to improve recognition accuracy and minimise verification errors.

Future development will focus on refining the feeding mechanism, improving the camera and lighting arrangement, and improving the efficiency of the conveyor system. Experimental testing will be required to evaluate singulation reliability, packaging safety, verification accuracy and processing time across different blister formats.

Finally, the initial prototype will be evaluated against the existing NUH Pharmacy restocking workflow to determine whether the proposed solution can reduce manual workload without compromising medication safety.


7. Conclusion

Through site visits, surveys and workflow analysis at NUH Pharmacy, the team identified repetitive manual counting and medication verification as key challenges in the existing DDS-2 restocking process. These findings guided the development of OptIVS, a proposed automated system integrating blister handling, counting and information verification.

Preliminary testing identified whole-image PaddleOCR as the best-performing recognition approach, achieving 68.1% character accuracy and 47.8% exact-match accuracy. Additionally, the horizontal conveyor concept was selected over the vertical drop-through mechanism due to having better-controlled blister positioning and suitability for integration with the inspection and rejection subsystems. These findings informed the development of the initial OptIVS prototype.

Although the proposed system has not yet achieved its target accuracy or undergone integrated validation, the interim results establish a foundation for further development. Future work will focus on improving text recognition accuracy, refining the mechanical mechanisms and integrating the subsystems. Subsequent testing with NUH Pharmacy will evaluate the system's potential to reduce manual workload and restocking time while maintaining medication safety.

Survey Introduction

We are a group of NUS students conducting this survey as part of our Final Year Project (FYP). We are exploring ways to develop a prototype that could support pharmacists during the DDS cartridge replenishment process, with the aim to improve workflow efficiency and accuracy.

This survey is intended to help us better understand the current workflow and identify areas where a prototype could provide useful support. We are not assessing individual performance, and there are no right or wrong answers. We will not collect any personal information, and we will use all responses only for our FYP.

B.3 Survey Data Analysis Method

The difficulty score for Question 2 was calculated using the weighted average rank of each task.

\[ \text{Weighted Average Rank} = \frac{n_1 + 2n_2 + 3n_3 + 4n_4}{N} \]

\[ \text{Difficulty Score} = 4 - \text{Weighted Average Rank} \]

Where \(n_1, n_2, n_3, n_4\) represent the number of respondents assigning ranks 1, 2, 3 and 4 respectively, and \(N\) is the total number of respondents. A higher difficulty score indicates a task perceived as more difficult.
